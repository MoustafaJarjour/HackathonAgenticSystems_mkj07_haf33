From a relationship to a working model

Initial-Rate Saturation: v = Vmax*S/(Km+S)

UnderstandExploreCheck the source

The concept

For a single-substrate enzyme at initial rate, the measured velocity v rises with substrate concentration S but saturates: v = Vmax*S/(Km+S). Vmax is the limiting rate approached at high S, and Km is the substrate concentration at which v is exactly half of Vmax. Because S and Km are both concentrations, their ratio S/Km is dimensionless, so the shape of the curve depends only on how S compares with Km.

Why it matters

This saturating rate law is the foundation of enzyme kinetics: it explains why reaction rate rises with substrate but plateaus once the enzyme is working at capacity, and it defines two measurable constants, Vmax and Km, that characterize an enzyme. Recognizing that S and Km share a concentration unit prevents unit-mismatch errors when fitting or comparing data, and the dimensionless ratio S/Km lets you predict the fractional saturation without knowing absolute concentrations.

Symbols and terms

v (nu)
Initial reaction rate (velocity) at a given substrate concentration.
Vmax
Limiting rate approached as substrate concentration becomes large; in the source this is the constant prefactor C*Phi.
Km
Substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax; in the source this is the constant k. Same concentration unit as S.
S ([S])
Substrate concentration, in the same concentration unit as Km.
S/Km
Dimensionless ratio S/Km that alone determines the fraction of Vmax attained.

How the mechanism works

  1. The rate law has a saturating form

    The 1913 invertase paper writes the initial rate as nu = C*Phi*[S]/([S]+k): the rate grows with substrate concentration [S] but is divided by [S] plus a constant k, so it cannot grow without bound.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: eq3

  2. What Vmax and Km mean

    Modern teaching notation renames the constant prefactor C*Phi as Vmax and the constant k as Km, giving v = Vmax*S/(Km+S). Vmax is the limiting rate at high S; Km is the substrate concentration at which v = Vmax/2. Because S and Km are added together, they must carry the same concentration unit.

    Teaching simplification Evidence: eq3

  3. Only the ratio S/Km sets the shape

    Dividing top and bottom by Km gives v = Vmax*(S/Km)/(1+S/Km). The rate therefore depends only on the dimensionless ratio S/Km: doubling both S and Km leaves v unchanged. This is why the curve's shape is set by how S compares with Km, not by absolute concentrations.

    Teaching simplification Evidence: eq3

Teaching simplification

Try these explorations

  1. Change: Set S to 0, then raise S gradually toward Km and beyond while keeping Vmax and Km fixed.

    Observe: At S = 0 the rate is 0; at S = Km the rate is exactly Vmax/2; as S grows well past Km the rate flattens toward Vmax.

    Why: The denominator Km+S makes the response hyperbolic: small S gives near-linear growth, and large S makes Km negligible so v approaches Vmax.

  2. Change: Double both S and Km together (for example S = 2, Km = 2, then S = 4, Km = 4) while holding Vmax fixed.

    Observe: The computed rate v and the fraction S/(Km+S) stay the same even though the absolute concentrations changed.

    Why: v depends only on the dimensionless ratio S/Km, so scaling both concentrations by the same factor leaves the rate unchanged; this is the practical reason S and Km must share a unit.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The demo evaluates only the algebraic initial-rate expression; it does not simulate the full time course of an enzyme reaction.
  • No experimental invertase data are reproduced here, so the plotted curve is illustrative of the rate law, not a fit to the 1913 measurements.
  • The model assumes a single substrate and ignores product inhibition, reversibility, and enzyme inactivation.
  • Km is treated as a given constant; the lesson does not derive it from individual rate constants.
  • This lesson evaluates the stated initial-rate saturation law. Renaming the historical constant k as Km is a teaching convention for this model; Km should not generally be interpreted as a binding dissociation constant.

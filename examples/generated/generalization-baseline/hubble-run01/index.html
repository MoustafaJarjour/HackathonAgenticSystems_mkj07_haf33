From a relationship to a working model

Hubble 1929: The Linear Distance–Velocity Relation as a First Approximation

UnderstandExploreCheck the source

The concept

Hubble's 1929 paper reports a linear correlation between distances and radial velocities of extra-galactic nebulae. The observed radial velocity v is modeled as a distance term plus three solar-motion terms; after removing the solar-motion terms, the simplified relation is v_corrected = d*K, where d is distance in Mpc and K is a historical slope in km/s per Mpc. The paper's fitted coefficients were 465 and 513, and it used the rounded value K=500. This lesson lets you sweep distance and slope to see the exact mean line, while stressing that real nebulae scatter about it and that the paper itself called the relation a first approximation over a restricted distance range.

Why it matters

This is the classic observational statement that galaxy recession velocity grows roughly with distance. It matters because it links a measurable spectrum shift to a distance estimate, giving a quantitative handle on cosmic expansion. It also teaches a key modeling habit: a clean linear law can be a useful first approximation whose residuals, selection effects, and limited range must be examined before drawing stronger conclusions.

Symbols and terms

d
Distance to a nebula in megaparsecs (Mpc); the source uses units of 10^6 parsecs.
K
Historical slope in km/s per Mpc. The paper's fitted values were 465 and 513, and it used the rounded value 500.
v_corrected
Radial velocity after removing the solar-motion terms, in km/s.
v
Observed radial velocity in km/s, including solar motion and residuals.
peculiar motion
A galaxy's own motion relative to the smooth expansion, which makes it deviate from the mean line.

How the mechanism works

  1. From observed velocity to a corrected distance term

    Hubble's equations of condition write the observed radial velocity as a distance term plus three terms describing the Sun's motion projected on the sky. The angular terms are removed to isolate the distance-dependent part, leaving the simplified teaching form v_corrected = d*K. Here d is distance in Mpc and K is the slope in km/s per Mpc.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: eq_condition, linear_correlation

  2. The historical slope is a rounded value

    The paper reports fitted coefficients of 465 and 513 km/s per million parsecs and then adopts the rounded intermediate value K=500. That number is a historical teaching value from 1929, not a present-day measurement of the Hubble constant. Changing K changes the predicted velocity at every distance.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: rounded_k, fitted_coeffs

  3. A first approximation with scatter

    The plotted corrected velocities still contain residuals, and the paper describes the linear relation as a first approximation over a restricted distance range. The exact line shown here is a simplified mean relation, not a fit to or reproduction of the observed nebular velocities. Real galaxies have peculiar motions, so not every point lies on the line.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: first_approx, residuals

Try these explorations

  1. Change: Keep K at 500 and move the distance slider from 2 Mpc to 4 Mpc.

    Observe: The predicted velocity doubles from 1000 km/s to 2000 km/s, and the ratio computation stays at 2.

    Why: At fixed K the relation is linear through the origin, so doubling distance doubles the predicted corrected velocity. This is the proportionality the paper's linear correlation suggests.

  2. Change: Set distance to 2 Mpc and raise K from 500 to 600.

    Observe: The predicted velocity rises from 1000 km/s to 1200 km/s, and the line view becomes steeper.

    Why: K is the slope of the line, so increasing K increases the predicted velocity at every fixed distance. The paper's fitted values 465 and 513 bracket the rounded 500, showing the slope was uncertain.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The exact line v = K*d is a simplified mean relation after solar-motion correction, not a fit to or reproduction of observed nebular velocities.
  • Real nebulae have peculiar motions and residuals, so individual galaxies scatter about the line.
  • The paper describes the linear relation as a first approximation over a restricted distance range; it should not be extrapolated relativistically.
  • K=500 is the paper's rounded historical teaching value, not a present-day measurement of the Hubble constant.
  • This lesson does not infer the age of the universe or claim the source established an exact modern cosmology.
  • The solar-motion terms are not modeled numerically here; only their removal is represented by the simplified form.

Our teaching simplifications

  • The simplified form v_corrected = d*K is a teaching construction after removing solar-motion terms; the source equation includes those terms explicitly.
  • Renaming r to d for the teaching example is a presentational choice.
  • The exact line view is a simplified mean relation, not a fit to or reproduction of observed nebular velocities.
  • The solar-motion terms X, Y, Z, alpha, and delta are not modeled numerically in this lesson.
  • The slider ranges and default values are pedagogical choices, not values taken from the paper.

Hubble 1929: The Linear Distance–Velocity Relation as a First Approximation

UnderstandExploreCheck the source

The concept

Hubble's 1929 paper reports a linear correlation between distances and radial velocities of extra-galactic nebulae. After the solar-motion terms are removed, the simplified relation is v_corrected = K·d, where d is distance in Mpc and K is a slope in km/s per Mpc. In the paper the fitted coefficients were 465 and 513 km/s per Mpc, and a rounded intermediate value K = 500 was used. This lesson lets you vary K and d to see the predicted corrected velocity and how the line steepens with K and scales with d. The displayed line is a simplified mean relation, not a fit to or reproduction of observed nebular velocities.

Why it matters

This relation is the observational seed of the expanding-universe idea: a roughly linear increase of recession velocity with distance. Understanding it as a first approximation over a restricted distance range, with scatter from peculiar motions, prevents over-reading it as an exact law or as a modern measurement of the Hubble constant.

Symbols and terms

d
Distance to a nebula in Mpc (millions of parsecs). In the paper this quantity is called r; the lesson renames it d.
K
Slope of the simplified corrected velocity–distance relation, in km/s per Mpc. Historical fitted values were 465 and 513; the paper used the rounded value 500.
v_corrected
Predicted corrected radial velocity in km/s after removing solar-motion terms, equal to K·d in this simplified teaching relation.
solar motion
The motion of the Sun through space, whose projection along a line of sight contributes angular terms X·cos(alpha)·cos(delta) + Y·sin(alpha)·cos(delta) + Z·sin(delta) that must be removed before the distance term is isolated.
peculiar motion
A galaxy's own motion relative to the mean expansion, which produces scatter about the mean line; the paper's corrected velocities still include residuals.

How the mechanism works

  1. A linear correlation in the data

    Hubble states that the tabulated data indicate a linear correlation between distances and velocities. The relation is written with solar-motion terms plus a distance term; after the angular solar-motion terms are removed, the simplified distance term is v_corrected = r·K. In this lesson we rename r to d and treat K as the slope in km/s per Mpc.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: linear_correlation, eq_condition, simplified_term

  2. Historical slope values and the rounded K

    The paper gives two fitted historical coefficients, 465 and 513 km/s per million parsecs, and then uses the rounded intermediate value K = 500. These are historical numbers, not current estimates of the Hubble constant. The slider default K = 500 is therefore a teaching value from the paper, not a present-day measurement.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: fitted_coeffs, rounded_k

  3. A first approximation over a restricted range

    The final paragraph calls the linear relation a first approximation over a restricted distance range. The plotted corrected velocities still include residuals, so the exact line shown here is a simplified mean relation, not a fit to or reproduction of observed nebular velocities. Do not extrapolate relativistically, infer a universe age, or claim this source established an exact modern cosmology.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: first_approx, residuals

Try these explorations

  1. Change: Set K = 500 and sweep d from 0 to 10 Mpc using the distance slider.

    Observe: The predicted corrected velocity v_corrected = K·d grows linearly from 0 to 5000 km/s, and the line view sweeps out a straight line through the origin.

    Why: This shows the simplified mean relation: at fixed K, doubling d doubles the predicted velocity, because v_corrected is directly proportional to d.

  2. Change: Keep d = 2 Mpc fixed and change K from 50 to 600 km/s per Mpc.

    Observe: The predicted velocity changes from 100 to 1200 km/s, and the line view becomes steeper as K increases.

    Why: K is the slope of the relation, so increasing K steepens the line and raises the predicted velocity at every fixed distance.

  3. Change: Compare the shown values v_at_k465, v_at_k513, and v_at_k500 at the same d.

    Observe: The three historical slopes give slightly different predicted velocities; the rounded K = 500 lies between the two fitted coefficients.

    Why: This illustrates that the paper reported fitted coefficients 465 and 513 and then used a rounded intermediate value 500, which is a historical teaching value rather than a modern measurement.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The displayed exact line is a simplified mean relation after solar-motion correction, not a fit to or reproduction of observed nebular velocities.
  • The plotted corrected velocities in the paper still include residuals; individual nebulae scatter about the mean line because of peculiar motions.
  • The paper calls the linear relation a first approximation over a restricted distance range; do not extrapolate it relativistically or to arbitrarily large distances.
  • K = 500 is the paper's rounded historical teaching value, not a present-day measurement of the Hubble constant.
  • This lesson does not infer a universe age or claim that the 1929 paper established an exact modern cosmology.
  • The sliders and exact predicted line are teaching constructions, not observational data.

Our teaching simplifications

  • Renaming the paper's distance symbol r to d for the teaching example.
  • Writing the simplified relation as v_corrected = d*K and v_corrected = K*d with the lesson's control ids.
  • Treating K as a single editable slope and d as a single editable distance, rather than fitting the paper's full equations of condition.
  • Displaying an exact straight line through the origin as a simplified mean relation; the paper's corrected velocities still include residuals and scatter.
  • Using K = 500 as a default teaching value from the paper's rounded historical number, not as a modern measurement of the Hubble constant.
  • The sliders and line view are teaching constructions, not observational data.

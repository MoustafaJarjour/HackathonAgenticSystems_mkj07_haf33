From Weights to Uncertainty: Shannon Entropy in Bits

UnderstandExploreCheck the source

The concept

Nonnegative weights are converted into a probability distribution by dividing each weight by the total weight. Shannon entropy H = -sum(p_i log2 p_i) measures the average uncertainty of that distribution in bits, with the convention that a zero-probability outcome contributes zero.

Why it matters

Entropy quantifies how many bits are needed on average to describe the outcome of a random source. It is the foundation of data compression and channel capacity: a source with entropy H bits per symbol cannot be compressed below H bits per symbol on average, and a uniform distribution over n outcomes has the maximum entropy log2(n).

Symbols and terms

w_i
Nonnegative weight for outcome i. Weights need not sum to 1.
W
Total weight, W = sum(w_i). Must be positive for normalization.
p_i
Probability of outcome i, computed as p_i = w_i / W.
H
Shannon entropy in bits, H = -sum(p_i log2 p_i), with 0 log2 0 treated as 0.
log2
Base-2 logarithm, so entropy is measured in bits.

How the mechanism works

  1. Normalize weights into probabilities

    Start with nonnegative weights w_i. Their sum W = sum(w_i) is positive. The normalized probabilities are p_i = w_i / W. This makes the p_i nonnegative and sum(p_i) = 1, so they form a valid probability distribution.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: entropy_formula, zero_contribution

  2. Entropy as average surprise

    For each outcome with probability p_i, the information content is -log2(p_i) bits. Shannon entropy is the probability-weighted average of these information contents: H = -sum(p_i log2 p_i). A certain outcome (p_i = 1) gives H = 0; a uniform distribution over n outcomes gives H = log2(n), the maximum for that n.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: entropy_formula, certainty_zero, zero_contribution

  3. Why the zero convention matters

    When p_i = 0, the term p_i log2(p_i) is undefined as a real number, but its limiting value is 0. Shannon's formula adopts the convention that a zero-probability outcome contributes 0 to the sum. This keeps H finite and continuous as probabilities approach zero.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: zero_contribution

See what changes

Try these explorations

  1. Change: Set the weights to [1, 1, 1] and then to [1, 0, 0].

    Observe: For [1, 1, 1], probabilities are [1/3, 1/3, 1/3] and H = log2(3) ≈ 1.585 bits. For [1, 0, 0], probabilities are [1, 0, 0] and H = 0 bits.

    Why: This shows the two extremes: maximum uncertainty for a uniform distribution over three outcomes, and zero uncertainty when the outcome is certain.

  2. Change: Keep the weights at [1, 1, 1] and vary the common scale factor from 0.1 to 10.

    Observe: The scaled weights change, but the probabilities remain [1/3, 1/3, 1/3] and H remains log2(3) ≈ 1.585 bits.

    Why: Normalization divides by the total weight, so multiplying all weights by the same positive constant cancels out. Entropy depends only on the relative proportions of the weights.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The demo uses exactly three outcomes to keep the visualization and calculations compact; the same formula applies to any finite number of outcomes.
  • The entropy formula assumes the probabilities are normalized to sum to 1. If the total weight is zero, normalization is undefined and entropy cannot be computed.
  • The convention 0 log2 0 = 0 is a limiting value, not a direct evaluation of the expression at zero.
  • This lesson does not derive the source coding theorem or channel capacity; it only illustrates the entropy formula and its basic properties.

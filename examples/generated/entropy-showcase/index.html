Entropy in Bits from Editable Outcome Weights

UnderstandExploreCheck the source

The concept

Entropy H measures the average uncertainty of a probability distribution over n outcomes. Given nonnegative weights w_i with positive total, we form probabilities p_i = w_i / sum(w) and compute H = -sum(p_i * log2(p_i)) in bits. H vanishes only when one outcome is certain, is positive otherwise, and for fixed n reaches its maximum log2(n) when all p_i are equal. Appending a zero weight adds an impossible outcome: it leaves H unchanged but raises the achievable maximum log2(n). Multiplying all weights by a positive common scale leaves the probabilities and H unchanged.

Why it matters

Entropy quantifies how many bits of choice or uncertainty a source has, which underlies data compression, channel capacity, and coding limits. Understanding that H depends only on the normalized probabilities, not on the raw weights or their scale, and that adding impossible outcomes changes the ceiling but not the current uncertainty, prevents common modeling errors when designing or analyzing communication systems.

H
Entropy in bits, H = -sum(p_i * log2(p_i)).
log2(n)
Maximum possible entropy for n outcomes, attained when all p_i are equal.
c
Positive common scale factor applied to every weight; cancels during normalization.

How the mechanism works

  1. From weights to probabilities

    The source defines H over probabilities p_i with sum(p_i)=1. To let you edit raw nonnegative weights, we normalize: p_i = w_i / sum(w), which requires a positive total. This division is a teaching input construction, not a quoted step. The entropy formula itself is the source's Theorem 2 in base-2 units.

    Teaching simplification Evidence: theorem2, sum_p_one

  2. Certainty and the uniform maximum

    If one outcome has all the probability, every term p_i*log2(p_i) is zero, so H = 0: only certainty makes H vanish, otherwise H is positive. For a fixed number of outcomes n, H is largest, equal to log2(n), when all p_i are equal. Both statements are source-supported properties.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: certainty, uniform_max

  3. Zero weights and common scaling

    A zero weight gives p_i = 0; by the continuity convention the term 0*log2(0) contributes zero, so appending a zero weight leaves H unchanged while raising the maximum log2(n). Multiplying every weight by the same positive factor c cancels in p_i = w_i/sum(w), so the probabilities and H are unchanged, while the scaled weights c*w_i and scaled total c*sum(w) both grow by the factor c. The zero-term convention and the scaling argument are teaching constructions.

    Teaching simplification Evidence: theorem2

Try these explorations

  1. Change: Set weights to [1,1,1,1] and read H; then resize to 5 outcomes (appending a zero) and read H again.

    Observe: H stays at 2 bits while log2(n) rises from 2 to about 2.322 bits.

    Why: A zero weight gives p_i = 0, whose entropy term is zero by the continuity convention, so H is unchanged; but the uniform maximum log2(n) grows with n.

  2. Change: Set weights to [1,1,1,1] and slide the common scale c from 1 to 10.

    Observe: The scaled weights and scaled total grow tenfold, but the probability bars and H remain identical.

    Why: The factor c cancels in p_i = w_i/sum(w), so normalization makes entropy invariant to a positive common scale.

Assumptions and limitations

  • This is a toy calculator over user-entered weights, not a reproduction of any experimental measurement.
  • The zero-term convention 0*log2(0)=0 and the weight-normalization step are teaching constructions, not quoted from the paper.
  • Only nonnegative weights with a positive total are valid; a zero total would make p_i undefined.
  • The demo covers at most 6 outcomes and does not model source coding or channel capacity.
  • Entropy values are computed from the entered weights only; no external data or fitted parameters are used.

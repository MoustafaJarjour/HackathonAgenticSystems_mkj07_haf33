Initial-Rate Kinetics: v = Vmax*S/(Km+S)

UnderstandExploreCheck the source

The concept

The 1913 initial-rate law for invertase action states that the reaction velocity is proportional to the enzyme amount and rises with substrate concentration toward a plateau. In modern notation v = Vmax*S/(Km+S): S and Km share one concentration unit, Vmax is the plateau rate reached as S grows, and Km is the substrate concentration giving exactly half of Vmax.

Why it matters

This rate law is the standard starting point for describing enzyme kinetics: it explains why reaction velocity rises with substrate but levels off, and it defines Km as a concentration that reports how much substrate is needed for half-maximal activity. Getting the units and the half-saturation reading right is essential before fitting real velocity data or comparing enzymes.

How the mechanism works

  1. The initial-rate law

    The 1913 paper writes the initial velocity as proportional to the enzyme amount Phi and to [S]/([S]+k). The modern teaching form v = Vmax*S/(Km+S) keeps that shape: Vmax = C*Phi collects the proportionality constant and enzyme amount, and Km = k is the substrate concentration at half saturation. Because S and Km are added, they must carry the same concentration unit.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: rate_law

  2. Why the curve saturates

    At small S the denominator is dominated by Km, so v is roughly (Vmax/Km)*S: the rate grows almost linearly with substrate. As S grows past Km the denominator is dominated by S, the fraction S/(Km+S) approaches 1, and v flattens toward the plateau Vmax. The gap Vmax - v shrinks but never reaches zero at finite S.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: rate_law

  3. Reading Km off the curve

    Setting S = Km makes the fraction Km/(Km+Km) = 1/2, so v = Vmax/2. This is the operational meaning of Km: it is read off the curve as the substrate concentration giving half the plateau rate, and it is a concentration, not a rate.

    Source-supported relationship Evidence: rate_law

Try these explorations

  1. Change: Set Km = 1 and Vmax = 1, then move S from 0 up to 20 in steps of about 1.

    Observe: v starts near 0, passes 0.5 at S = 1, and by S = 8 is about 0.89 while the gap Vmax - v has fallen to about 0.11.

    Why: The fraction S/(Km+S) rises steeply while S < Km and then bends over, so the rate approaches the plateau without ever exceeding it.

  2. Change: Keep S = 4 fixed and raise Km from 0.1 to 10.

    Observe: v falls from about 0.976 toward about 0.286 as Km grows, and the saturation fraction S/(Km+S) falls with it.

    Why: Km sits in the denominator, so a larger Km means more substrate is needed to reach the same fraction of the plateau; at fixed S the enzyme looks less saturated.

Assumptions and limitations

  • This is a toy evaluation of the rate law, not a reproduction of the 1913 invertase experiments; no measured velocities or concentrations from the paper are used.
  • The excerpt supplies only the transcribed equation and the statement that C is a proportionality constant, so the identification Vmax = C*Phi and Km = k is a teaching interpretation.
  • Km is not generally a binding dissociation constant; it is the substrate concentration giving half-maximal rate under the stated initial-rate assumptions and can reflect multiple kinetic steps.
  • The model assumes a single substrate, initial-rate conditions, and no product inhibition, reversibility, or enzyme inactivation; those effects are outside the excerpt.
  • The line view sweeps S only up to the slider maximum of 20, so the plateau is approached but never exactly attained.
